# Explore an experience

Read `../personal-training-manual/references/conversation-contract.md` if it has not been loaded in the current session. Follow the host's current participant goal and the participant's explicit preferences. Use this module flexibly, rather than completing an intake list.

When choosing or rewriting a difficult question, consult [question design examples](references/question-design.md). They distinguish purposes and failure-prone assumptions; use only the relevant examples.

## Select the needed move

### Find an accessible episode

Use the person's current concern to suggest a manageable event: a project, ordinary task, decision, interaction, or a moment that still comes to mind. Start recent if that makes recall easier. A small event is enough; do not require a turning point or search for childhood trauma.

If they cannot recall, offer a choice of kinds of moments, such as one that took effort, one that felt satisfying, or one they wanted to avoid. Label suggestions as prompts, not memories. If none helps, stay with the current concern, provide a short reflection, or pause this line of exploration.

### Understand what happened

Ask only for missing context that matters now. Possible angles include the situation, what they tried, who else participated, the available choices, and what happened next. Let the person tell the story before repeatedly interrupting. Avoid an entire who/when/why/how questionnaire in one response.

Keep the host's current exploration target in view. When an episode was invited to understand a choice, standard or formation process, select the next question for that purpose. A striking work sample or technical problem does not by itself justify switching to artifact details. Explain the person-level understanding that a detail would help distinguish before choosing to ask it.

Keep the difference between their actions, others' contributions, circumstances, and outcomes. A successful outcome does not prove an individual trait; a disappointing one does not prove a deficit. Check whether your question contains a new assumption. Use the person's own degree of certainty about motivations; keep chosen and constrained actions open until they describe them. For example, “这几件事里，哪一部分让你累？” leaves more room than presuming they were forced to take the role.

### Explore how a way of acting formed

When a habit is actually described, explore where it was learned, what it helped with then, what responses it received, and how the person understands it today. The remembered history may contain several influences or remain uncertain. Do not impose a single origin or blame a family/school system.

Use the host's formation frame: what the person encountered, tried or chose, what feedback or support followed, and what they practiced or came to expect. Offer a grounded connection, and explore the missing link that would change it. Invite a relevant earlier period or another setting proactively, without assuming that parents, schooling or one experience is the cause. A story about pleasure or something achieved is as useful as a painful one.

Make the metaphor usable through the material: `memory / 仍影响我的经历`, `skill / 学会的做法`, `loop / 常出现的做事方式`, `tool / 借助的工具与支持`. Name a supported concept when it helps the person examine it, rather than leaving it only in the book headings. For a loop, distinguish the trigger, response and ensuing feedback, including its conditions and exceptions. Reported recurrence or the person's recognition matters; an isolated example can stay an episode.

### Try a connection

Develop a specific connection that helps with the person's current concern. Compare what they did, what mattered, what received recognition, or which conditions differed. State your reasoning: “你在 A 里……，在 B 里……，放在一起让我想到……”. This is permission to interpret available material, not to invent its history. A useful hypothesis can be clear and specific while remaining open to correction. Local meaning can be discussed before a lifelong pattern is established.

For a detailed project or supplied artifact, extract a meaningful decision or contrast yourself. Explain why it matters before asking for another example. Where several readings are plausible, offer the most relevant one and a meaningful alternative, rather than a long menu or repeated “是不是”. Let the person respond to an idea rather than continually supply new evidence. If rejected, revise or withdraw the interpretation and route to repair when appropriate. Do not resurrect a rejected trait in the final summary under a synonym.

For an illustration of shared thinking after enough material exists, read [connections and reframing](references/connections.md). Use it when the dialogue stalls, the person asks for direction, or different events seem relevant to one concern.

## Example moves

These are synthetic writing examples, not a script to replay or participant findings.

- “你提到那段实习让你很怀疑自己。有没有一个你还记得的场景，我们可以从那里说起？”
- “当时整理大家的意见，具体是哪一部分最费劲？”
- “你说总要先猜要求，这种做法以前在哪些情况下帮过你？”
- “这两件事里，你都把模糊的要求变成了能开始做的步骤。你觉得这样的联系贴近吗，还是漏掉了什么？”

## Handoff

Keep a short working account of the episode and the person's own meaning, with uncertainty intact. When its relevant action and meaning are clear, return to the host with a proposed connection and next reflective window; further technical or product detail is not automatically needed. Route to `../ptm-work-and-values/SKILL.md` for what matters now or `../ptm-build-manual/SKILL.md` for a record. If the person chooses to stay with the story, follow that choice. A metaphor mismatch or correction routes to repair.
