## 2. Correction made after reading Booth

Booth’s three-step formula separates conceptual significance from later practical use:

1. **Topic:** I am studying...
2. **Question:** because I want to find out...
3. **Significance:** in order to help my audience understand...

The earlier draft moved too quickly from the question to what designers should do. The revised Step 3 first states what the research audience may understand. Possible design use comes after that.

### Final three-step version

**Topic:** I am studying how adult Chinese Gen Z understand their own role and the role of digital systems across super apps, generative-AI chat, and emerging AI agents.

**Question:** I want to find out how their expectations change when a system shapes an everyday routine, participates in knowledge work, or acts on their behalf.

**Significance:** I want to help interaction design and HCI audiences understand how people define human–system roles across these different forms of involvement, and how the highly integrated Chinese digital context shapes those definitions.

**Possible practical use:** This understanding may help designers make system roles easier for people to notice, discuss, and reshape.

## 3. Reading notes for group discussion

### Prologue: Planning Your Project

- Research develops through writing, not only through reading first and writing later.
- Short summaries, diagrams, disagreements, and questions can help a researcher discover what they think.
- A useful project explanation includes the question, a possible answer, and the evidence that may support it.
- Discussion with other researchers can expose unclear parts of the project early.

**Connection to this thesis:** The current project still has a working answer rather than a conclusion. The probes and participant interviews will test whether the three-part comparison actually makes sense to participants.

### Chapter 3: From Topics to Questions

- A topic gives a place to begin, but a research project needs a question.
- Narrowing a topic does not only mean choosing a smaller object. The researcher must identify something they do not yet understand.
- The three-step sentence connects the topic, the unknown question, and its significance for an audience.
- The “So what?” step may remain provisional at the beginning, but it cannot remain missing at the end.

**Connection to this thesis:** “Chinese Gen Z and AI” is only a topic. The research question identifies the unknown: how participants define the roles of people and systems across three different arrangements.

### Chapter 4: From Questions to Problems

- A conceptual problem has a condition and a consequence. The condition is something we do not understand. The consequence is the more important understanding that remains blocked.
- Practical and conceptual problems should not be joined through a weak or invented claim.
- A project can first establish conceptual significance and then state a possible practical use.
- The wording of a question can create bias by excluding possible answers.

**Connection to this thesis:** The question should not assume that technology always removes agency. It must leave room for participants to value assistance, accept dependence, reject a role, or describe mixed feelings.

## 4. Seven-to-ten-minute presentation plan

### Slide 1 — Topic and personal starting point (45 seconds)

**Title:** Human–System Roles in the Digital Lives of Chinese Gen Z

- I often treat an app as a tool I choose.
- The same system can also change what feels normal or necessary.
- My project begins with this tension.

### Slide 2 — The topic becomes a question (55 seconds)

Show the three-step Booth structure:

- Topic: adult Chinese Gen Z and digital systems.
- Unknown: how people define their role and the system’s role.
- Significance: understanding how these roles change across different forms of system involvement.

### Slide 3 — Main research question (50 seconds)

Display only the bold research question and briefly explain the verbs:

- “Understand” means how participants describe the current role.
- “Negotiate” includes accepting, questioning, resisting, or revising that role.

### Slide 4 — Three selected system arrangements (75 seconds)

- Super apps shape everyday routines.
- Generative-AI chat participates in knowledge work.
- AI agents may act on a person’s behalf.

Explain that these cases limit the scope. They do not represent every part of life.

### Slide 5 — Why the Chinese context matters (60 seconds)

- Super apps connect communication, payment, services, work, and institutions.
- Participation can depend on social and institutional expectations, not only personal choice.
- The study focuses on adult Chinese Gen Z without claiming to represent the entire generation.

### Slide 6 — What existing research already offers (70 seconds)

- Human–AI research often studies a defined task.
- Existing guidance covers explanation, correction, feedback, and user control.
- This study asks how people define the system’s place before deciding which feature to add.

### Slide 7 — Research through Design and three probes (95 seconds)

- *Evil PM Simulator*: platform decisions and everyday routines.
- *Epistemic Illusion*: AI participation in thinking and knowledge production.
- *Cyber-Utopia Persona Quiz*: delegation across simple and sensitive situations.

Explain that the probes create situations for participants to react to. They do not teach one correct view of technology.

### Slide 8 — Expected contribution and open questions (70 seconds)

- Situated findings from adult Chinese Gen Z.
- A revised model of human–system roles.
- Design considerations grounded in the three arrangements.

End with two questions for feedback:

1. Does the comparison across the three arrangements feel coherent?
2. Which boundary of the participant group needs a clearer definition?

**Estimated speaking time:** approximately 8 minutes and 40 seconds.
