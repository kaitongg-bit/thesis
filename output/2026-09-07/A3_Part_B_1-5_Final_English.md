# A3 Part B: Empirical Research and Impact Model

**Working title:** *Trojan Horse: Provoking Reflection on Human Agency with Chinese Gen Z across Super Apps, Generative-AI Chat Interfaces, and AI Agents*

## 1. Problems in empirical design research

Cantamessa reviewed 718 papers from two engineering design conferences. He found 111 papers that reported empirical studies. Blessing and Chakrabarti use this review to show several common problems.

1. Many papers do not report the sample size. Cantamessa found this in 41% of the empirical papers.
2. Some papers do not state the unit of analysis. In 22% of the papers, readers cannot tell what was studied: a person, a behaviour, a design task, or something else.
3. Some papers do not state their research approach. This happened in 10% of the papers.
4. Some papers do not explain what their findings mean. This happened in 25% of the papers.
5. Methods are often unclear. A paper may say how data was collected, but not give enough context or explain how the data was analysed.
6. The parts of a study may not match. The research question, method, analysis, and conclusion may point in different directions.
7. Findings, assumptions, interpretations, and conclusions are often mixed together. This makes it hard to know what the data really supports.

This matters for my project. I need to separate published evidence, my own assumptions, and questions that are still open. In my models, I use `[R]` for literature, `[A]` for assumptions, and `[?]` for unknown relationships.

## 2. Initial Impact Model, criteria, and factors

My project has one Key Factor and three supports. The Key Factor is:

> **Recognition of how digital systems shape ways of living, knowing, and acting.**

I do not mean that participants must dislike technology. I ask whether they notice that a system is not only a neutral tool. It can also shape daily routines, knowledge work, and real-life action.

| Support | What it does | Expected immediate effect `[A]` |
|---|---|---|
| **Evil PM Simulator** | Uses a role shift. The player becomes a product manager and builds a fictional super app under KPI pressure. | The player may notice how a platform can organise daily routines and social habits. |
| **Epistemic Illusion** | Uses AI support and then an interruption during a detective game. | The player may notice how AI takes part in forming knowledge and judgement. |
| **Cyber-Utopia Persona Quiz** | Turns future-life quiz answers into agent rules, then runs these rules in difficult edge cases. | The player may notice that rules cannot fully describe real relationships and changing situations. |

All three supports point to the same Key Factor. In the model, every new arrow is marked `[A]`. This means it is a design assumption, not a proven result.

| DRM term | My project |
|---|---|
| **Success Factor** | A participant can clearly say how they want a digital system to take part in one specific situation. |
| **Success Criterion** | The participant can describe a preferred relationship with the system, instead of only accepting it as normal. |
| **Measurable Success Factor** | The participant can connect one system arrangement to one real consequence in life, knowledge, or action. |
| **Measurable Success Criterion** | The participant names: 1) a system arrangement, 2) its consequence, and 3) what they want to keep, change, question, or refuse. |

For example: “The agent can search for jobs, but it should not submit an application for me. It does not know whether I am willing to move to that city.” This is not a test of whether the participant likes AI. It shows that they can explain a preferred relationship with the system.

## 3. Proposed intervention, success criteria, and factors

This thesis looks at how adult Chinese Gen Z participants live with digital systems. Super apps are part of daily life. They connect chat, payment, shopping, services, and information. Generative-AI chat tools can help people search, write, explain, and make judgements. AI agents may later act for people in daily life.

I do not start from the idea that technology always reduces human agency. These systems can save time and help people do things that are hard to do alone. However, people may also treat them as simple tools. They may not notice that the systems shape how they live, think, and act.

I will make three Trojan Horse probes. They look like familiar online forms: a management game, a detective game, and a personality quiz. They are not designed to teach one correct answer. They create a small moment of discomfort or surprise. After this moment, participants can explain what they noticed, disagree with the probe, or change their earlier choices.

The first probe is *Evil PM Simulator*. The player starts as a user, then becomes a product manager. The player must improve activity, clicks, and revenue. To do this, they add features such as payment, social tools, shopping, data collection, notifications, and default settings to a fictional super app. The game does not only say that dark patterns are bad. It asks how a simple app becomes something that people need for daily life. A player may notice that their habits, group chat, payment, and recommendations feel normal, but are also shaped by platform goals.

The second probe is *Epistemic Illusion*. It is a detective game. The player can ask an AI assistant to sort clues, suggest ideas, and remove suspects. Near the final answer, the AI is no longer available. The game then shows what the AI did and what the player did. The player can talk about what they understood, what they checked, and what they only accepted. The AI interruption does not prove that AI makes people less capable. Some participants may say that AI helped them. Others may say that they lost track of the reasoning. Both kinds of answers are useful.

The third probe is *Cyber-Utopia Persona Quiz*. At first, it looks like a fun future-life test. Participants choose how much help they want from an AI agent with schedules, social invitations, job searching, and emotional support. The system turns these answers into rules. It then shows a future situation where the agent follows the rule exactly. The situation is an edge case. For example, an agent may follow a rule about social invitations but miss a hidden meaning in a message from a close friend. The point is not that the agent breaks the rule. The point is that life has relationships and exceptions that are hard to write into a rule in advance. Participants can keep, change, pause, or reject their earlier rule.

The Key Factor is whether participants notice how digital systems shape living, knowing, and acting. The Success Factor is whether they can clearly say what kind of relationship they want with a system in one situation. The Measurable Success Factor is whether they can connect a system arrangement to a real consequence. I will look for three things in their answers: a concrete arrangement, a concrete consequence, and a preferred response.

I will collect choices made in the probes, revisions, short annotations, and post-probe interviews. I will not give participants an agency score. I will look for different views. A participant may want more delegation. Another may want more control. Someone may say the real problem is work pressure or lack of alternatives, not interface design. These answers will help me revise the Reference Model. The final result will be an annotated probe portfolio and a revised model. It will not claim that three short games can permanently change people’s lives.

## 4. Relationships, practices, and ways of being

Escobar asks designers to think about relationships and ways of being, not only things. This helps me see my project in a different way. My three probes are not only games. They show three relationships between people and digital systems.

In *Evil PM Simulator*, the system becomes part of daily life and social habits. In *Epistemic Illusion*, AI becomes part of thinking and knowledge work. In *Cyber-Utopia Persona Quiz*, an agent becomes part of action, relationships, and care.

The probes ask people to notice, question, and explain these relationships. They can say that a system is useful, necessary, uncomfortable, or all of these at the same time. They can also say that my questions are too simple. This is important. I do not want to decide in advance what a good life with technology should be. I want participants to show where my model is missing something.

## 5. Research questions

1. How do adult Chinese Gen Z participants describe the ways super apps shape daily routines, social expression, and practical dependence?
2. How do participants describe the role of generative AI in their understanding, judgement, and sense of knowledge ownership?
3. When an AI agent follows a participant’s rule in an edge case, how does the participant explain, change, or defend that rule?
4. What conditions make a digital system feel helpful, necessary, shaping, or hard to refuse?
5. How do participants challenge or add to my first ideas about how digital systems shape living, knowing, and acting?
