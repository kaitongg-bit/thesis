# A3 Part B (2–5): Initial Impact Model and Research Plan

**Working title:** *Trojan Horse: Provoking Reflection on Human Agency with Chinese Gen Z across Super Apps, Generative-AI Chat Interfaces, and AI Agents*

## 2. Initial Impact Model, criteria, and evaluation focus

The accompanying Initial Impact Model is derived from the Initial Reference Model. It makes one design argument rather than three unrelated arguments. It contains **one Key Factor and three supports**. The supports use different mechanisms—perspective shift, dependency disruption, and edge-case deployment—but all address the same Key Factor: recognition of how digital systems shape ways of living, knowing, and acting. Each support is represented by a hexagon, as required in DRM.

| Support | Function and implementation | Immediate anticipated effect `[A]` | Link to the shared Key Factor `[A]` |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | Support recognition of super-app infrastructural shaping through perspective shift: *Evil PM Simulator* | Recognition of platform organisation of everyday routines | Makes visible one way a digital system shapes living. |
| H2 | Support reflection on AI-mediated knowledge formation through dependency disruption: *Epistemic Illusion* | Recognition of AI participation in knowledge formation | Makes visible one way a digital system shapes knowing. |
| H3 | Support reflection on agentic delegation through edge-case deployment: *Cyber-Utopia Persona Quiz* | Recognition of limits of pre-specified delegation in lived contexts | Makes visible one way a digital system shapes acting. |

| DRM term | Choice for this project | Desired value | How it will be assessed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Key Factor** | Recognition of how digital systems shape ways of living, knowing, and acting | Higher recognition in a situated probe encounter | Participant identifies that a system is more than a neutral tool and describes a shaping relation. |
| **Success Factor** | Clarity of a participant-defined preferred relationship with a digital system in a specific context | Clearer account of how the system should and should not take part | Participant states what they would retain, share, delegate, question, or refuse, and why. |

All arrows in the Impact Model are marked `[A]`: they are design assumptions that the project will examine. The open debrief, withdrawal option, and opportunity to annotate or challenge the work are research and ethics procedures. They are not a fourth Trojan Horse support.

## 3. Proposed intervention, measurable success criteria, and factors

This thesis investigates how adult Chinese Gen Z participants experience digital systems that increasingly take part in everyday life. Super-apps can organise social communication, payment, consumption, mobility, and information within connected infrastructures. Generative-AI chat interfaces can take part in searching, drafting, explaining, and judging. Emerging AI agents may act on a person’s behalf in everyday life. The project does not assume that these systems simply reduce human agency. They can provide convenience, assistance, and access. The concern is that people may treat them as neutral tools even when they also shape how people live, know, and act.

The project develops three satirical serious-game probes under the title Trojan Horse. They are disguised as familiar internet forms: a management simulator, a detective game, and a personality quiz. Their function is not to provide an efficient solution or to persuade participants that technology is harmful. Their function is to make the shaping role of digital systems felt and discussable. Before participation, people will be told that the materials contain constructed scenarios and that the detailed design intent will be discussed afterward. Participants may skip questions, challenge the framing, and request withdrawal or deletion of their material after the debrief.

The first prototype, *Evil PM Simulator*, concerns super-app infrastructure. A player shifts from being a user to being a product manager under pressure to grow activity, clicks, and revenue. To meet these targets, the player combines social interaction, payment, consumption, data collection, attention prompts, and default settings into a fictional super-app. The point is not merely to identify isolated dark patterns. It is to make tangible how a simple tool can become an environment that people must learn to inhabit. A participant may recognise that payment, group chat, recommendations, or expressive habits feel personal and ordinary while also being organised by platform priorities and practical dependence.

The second prototype, *Epistemic Illusion*, concerns generative-AI knowledge work. In a detective-style task, players are encouraged to rely on an AI assistant to sort clues, generate hypotheses, and eliminate suspects. At the final deduction, the AI becomes unavailable. The game then exposes a contribution trace and asks participants to reflect on what they understood, accepted, checked, or skipped. The interruption is a designed condition, not evidence that AI necessarily weakens knowledge or ownership. Participants may report dependence, confusion, useful collaboration, or no loss of understanding. The probe examines how AI can shape the process through which people form and claim knowledge.

The third prototype, *Cyber-Utopia Persona Quiz*, concerns emerging agentic delegation. It begins as an appealing MBTI-style test about an ideal AI-supported future life. Participants choose preferences about scheduling, social invitations, job searching, emotional support, and convenience. The system then deploys those preferences as agent rules in simulated everyday situations. It faithfully applies the participant’s earlier rule, including in an edge case where the rule becomes insufficient or uncomfortable. The point is not to show that the agent has made a mistake. It is to make visible that relationships, tacit meanings, and changing circumstances cannot always be fully specified in advance. Participants can keep, revise, suspend, or reject their earlier rule and explain why.

The Key Factor is participants’ recognition of how systems shape ways of living, knowing, and acting. The Success Factor is the clarity of a participant-defined preferred relationship with a system in a specific context. The Measurable Success Factor is the ability to connect a concrete system arrangement to a lived consequence. The measurable success criterion is met when a participant account includes three elements: a concrete arrangement, such as a default setting, AI contribution, or delegated agent rule; a consequence for their life, knowledge, or action; and a preferred response, relation, or condition. For example, a participant may say that an agent can collect job information but must not submit an application because location preferences and personal circumstances cannot be inferred reliably.

Evidence will include in-probe choices, revisions, annotations, and post-probe interviews. The study will not calculate a single agency score. It will analyse whether participants recognise shaping relations, what they consider acceptable or unacceptable, and what conditions lead them to revise, defend, or reject a system arrangement. It will also record counterexamples: participants may prefer delegation, find a system genuinely helpful, or identify institutional pressure rather than interface design as the main constraint. The outcome is an annotated probe portfolio and a revised Reference Model, not a claim that the prototypes have permanently changed participants’ lives.

## 4. Designing relationships, practices, and ways of being

Escobar challenges this project to move beyond treating design as the production of separate things. The thesis asks how tools become infrastructures, cognitive partners, and agents that take part in making everyday worlds. *Evil PM Simulator* makes participants confront how platform priorities can organise routines and social norms. *Epistemic Illusion* makes knowledge formation a relationship among a person, an AI system, and an incomplete process. *Cyber-Utopia Persona Quiz* makes delegation a relationship that must face context, exception, and responsibility.

The practices I am designing are practices of noticing, interpreting, questioning, and revising. Participants can treat a system as helpful, troubling, necessary, or all three at once. They can also challenge the categories offered by a probe and identify constraints that design alone cannot remove. This does not mean that every preference is equally unconstrained, or that a configurable interface creates a pluriverse. It means the research must remain open to different ways of living with digital systems and to the unequal conditions that shape those possibilities.

## 5. Research questions

1. How do adult Chinese Gen Z participants describe the ways super-app infrastructures shape everyday routines, social expression, and practical dependence?
2. How do participants describe the role of generative-AI collaboration in shaping their understanding, judgement, and sense of knowledge ownership?
3. When an AI agent faithfully applies a participant’s stated preference in an edge case, how do participants explain, revise, or defend that preference?
4. What contextual conditions do participants cite when describing a system as helpful, necessary, shaping, or difficult to refuse?
5. How do participants challenge, revise, or add to the researcher’s initial account of how digital systems shape living, knowing, and acting?
